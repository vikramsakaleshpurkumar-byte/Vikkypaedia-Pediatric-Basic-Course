*Frederick J. Suchy and Amy G. Feldman*

## OVERVIEW AND EPIDEMIOLOGY
**Diseases of the gallbladder** in infants, children, and adolescents include congenital anomalies (gallbladder agenesis, bilobed/ectopic gallbladder), **gallbladder hydrops**, **cholelithiasis (gallstones)**, **calculous and acalculous cholecystitis**, and biliary dyskinesia.

## CLINICAL MANIFESTATIONS AND PATHOPHYSIOLOGY
**Cholelithiasis** in children comprises three stone types: (1) **Black pigment stones** (~40–50% of pediatric gallstones; composed of calcium bilirubinate polymers, classically caused by **chronic hemolytic anemias** [sickle cell disease, hereditary spherocytosis, pyruvate kinase deficiency, thalassemia major], ileal Crohn disease/resection, total parenteral nutrition, or **ceftriaxone pseudolithiasis** [reversible calcium-ceftriaxone biliary sludge/precipitates]); (2) **Cholesterol stones** (increasingly prevalent in **adolescent females with obesity**, pregnancy, oral contraceptive use, or *ABCB4* [MDR3] variants); and (3) **Brown pigment stones** (associated with bacterial/parasitic biliary stasis). **Acute acalculous cholecystitis** accounts for **30–50% of acute cholecystitis in children** (far higher than in adults!) and occurs during severe systemic illness, sepsis, trauma/burns, **Kawasaki disease (gallbladder hydrops)**, typhoid fever (*Salmonella* Typhi), EBV, or streptococcal infection. **Biliary colic** manifests as episodic right upper quadrant or epigastric pain radiating to the right scapula after fatty meals, whereas **acute cholecystitis** causes persistent RUQ pain (>6 hours), fever, leukocytosis, and a positive **Murphy sign**.

## DIAGNOSIS AND DIFFERENTIAL EVALUATION
**Right upper quadrant ultrasound** is >95% sensitive for gallstones (echogenic foci with posterior acoustic shadowing), biliary sludge, gallbladder wall thickening (>3.5 mm), pericholecystic fluid, and common bile duct dilatation. **gallbladder hydrops** (marked distension without stones, wall inflammation, or congenital malformation, classically seen in **Kawasaki disease** or streptococcal pharyngitis) is readily diagnosed on ultrasound. **MRCP** or endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) evaluates suspected **choledocholithiasis** (elevated conjugated bilirubin, GGT, and amylase/lipase).

#### **Table 414.1** Key Clinical, Diagnostic, and Therapeutic Summary for Diseases of the Gallbladder
| Clinical Entity / Parameter | Pathophysiology, Diagnosis & Management |
| --- | --- |
| **Black Pigment Gallstones** | Calcium bilirubinate; chronic hemolysis (sickle cell disease, hereditary spherocytosis, thalassemia), TPN, ileal resection; radiopaque in ~50% |
| **Ceftriaxone Biliary Pseudolithiasis** | Reversible calcium-ceftriaxone precipitates in gallbladder; resolves spontaneously within weeks of stopping ceftriaxone; surgery NOT indicated |
| **Gallbladder Hydrops in Pediatrics** | Acute acalculous non-inflammatory distension of gallbladder; classic association with Kawasaki disease and group A streptococcal infection; self-resolving with medical therapy |
| **Symptomatic Cholelithiasis / Choledocholithiasis** | Laparoscopic cholecystectomy for symptomatic stones or hemolytic disease; ERCP stone extraction for common bile duct obstruction |

## TREATMENT AND PROGNOSIS
**Asymptomatic gallstones** in otherwise healthy children (and **ceftriaxone-induced biliary pseudolithiasis** or **Kawasaki gallbladder hydrops**) are managed **expectantly without surgery**, as neonatal and ceftriaxone stones frequently resolve spontaneously. **Symptomatic cholelithiasis**, **calculous cholecystitis**, and **children with sickle cell disease or hereditary spherocytosis with gallstones** (frequently performed concomitantly with splenectomy in hereditary spherocytosis) are treated with **laparoscopic cholecystectomy**. **Choledocholithiasis** is managed with **preoperative or postoperative ERCP with sphincterotomy and stone extraction** alongside laparoscopic cholecystectomy.
