## OVERVIEW AND EPIDEMIOLOGY
**Peritonitis**—inflammation of the parietal and visceral peritoneum—is classified clinically and microbiologically into **Acute Primary (Spontaneous) Bacterial Peritonitis (Subchapter 419.1)**, **Acute Secondary Peritonitis (Subchapter 419.2)** from hollow viscus perforation, and **Acute Secondary Localized Peritonitis / Intra-abdominal Abscess (Subchapter 419.3)**.

## CLINICAL MANIFESTATIONS AND PATHOPHYSIOLOGY
**Subchapter 419.1: Primary Peritonitis (Spontaneous Bacterial Peritonitis [SBP])** is an acute bacterial infection of pre-existing ascitic fluid **without an intra-abdominal surgical source**, occurring predominantly in children with **nephrotic syndrome** or **decompensated cirrhosis / biliary atresia** via hematogenous or transmural bacterial translocation. The classic pathogens are **monomicrobial**: **Streptococcus pneumoniae** (historically #1 in nephrotic syndrome) and **Gram-negative enteric bacilli (*Escherichia coli*, *Klebsiella pneumoniae*)** (#1 in cirrhotic ascites). Children present with fever, diffuse abdominal pain, vomiting, rebound tenderness, ileus, or unexplained worsening of encephalopathy/renal function in cirrhosis. **Subchapter 419.2: Secondary Peritonitis** arises from perforation of the GI tract (**perforated appendicitis** [#1 in children], **necrotizing enterocolitis [NEC]** in neonates, Meckel diverticulitis, Peptic ulcer, or typhoid perforation) and is **polymicrobial** (enteric Gram-negative rods **E. coli**, *Klebsiella*, **Enterococcus**, and anaerobes **Bacteroides fragilis**). **Subchapter 419.3: Peritoneal Abscess** localizes to the pelvis, right lower quadrant, or subphrenic space, manifesting 5–10 days after perforated appendicitis with spiking 'picket-fence' fevers, leukocytosis, and tenesmus/diarrhea (pelvic abscess).

## DIAGNOSIS AND DIFFERENTIAL EVALUATION
In **SBP (Primary Peritonitis)**, **diagnostic paracentesis** BEFORE starting antibiotics is mandatory: an **ascitic fluid absolute neutrophil count (PMN) ≥250 cells/mm³** establishes the diagnosis and mandates immediate empiric treatment (inoculate ascitic fluid directly into blood culture bottles at the bedside to maximize yield). If ascitic fluid is polymicrobial, has total protein >1 g/dL, glucose <50 mg/dL, and markedly elevated LDH, suspect **secondary peritonitis (hollow viscus perforation)** and obtain upright/decubitus radiographs or CT for **free intraperitoneal air (pneumoperitoneum)**.

#### **Table 419.1** Key Clinical, Diagnostic, and Therapeutic Summary for Peritonitis
| Clinical Entity / Parameter | Pathophysiology, Diagnosis & Management |
| --- | --- |
| **Acute Primary Peritonitis / SBP (Subchapter 419.1)** | Nephrotic syndrome or cirrhosis; monomicrobial (S. pneumoniae, E. coli, Klebsiella); ascitic PMN ≥250 cells/mm³; treat with IV Ceftriaxone/Cefotaxime (medical, non-surgical) |
| **Acute Secondary Peritonitis (Subchapter 419.2)** | Perforated appendicitis (#1 in children) or neonatal NEC; polymicrobial (E. coli + Bacteroides fragilis); free air on imaging; urgent surgical source control + Pip-Tazo or Ceftriaxone/Metronidazole |
| **Localized Peritoneal Abscess (Subchapter 419.3)** | Pelvic, interloop, or subphrenic abscess post-appendicitis; spiking fevers & tenesmus; US/CT-guided percutaneous drainage + IV antibiotics |
| **CAPD (Peritoneal Dialysis) Peritonitis** | Cloudy dialysate effluent with WBC >100/mm³ (>50% PMNs); Staphylococcus epidermidis or S. aureus (#1) or Pseudomonas; intraperitoneal vancomycin + ceftazidime/cefepime |

## TREATMENT AND PROGNOSIS
**Primary Peritonitis (SBP)** is treated medically (**no surgery**) with **intravenous third-generation cephalosporin (cefotaxime or ceftriaxone)** for 7–10 days, plus **IV albumin** in cirrhotic patients to prevent hepatorenal syndrome. **Secondary Peritonitis** requires aggressive isotonic crystalloid fluid resuscitation, broad-spectrum **polymicrobial IV antimicrobial coverage** (**piperacillin-tazobactam**, OR **ceftriaxone + metronidazole**, OR meropenem), and **urgent surgical source control** (laparotomy/laparoscopy, appendectomy, bowel resection, or peritoneal lavage). **Localized Intra-abdominal Abscesses (>3 cm)** are managed with **CT- or ultrasound-guided percutaneous catheter drainage** plus antibiotics.
