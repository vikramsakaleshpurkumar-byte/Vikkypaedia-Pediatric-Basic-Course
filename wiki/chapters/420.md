*John J. Aiken*

## OVERVIEW AND EPIDEMIOLOGY
**Epigastric hernia** and **incisional hernia** (**Subchapter 420.1**) are defects of the anterior abdominal wall aponeurotic fascia that present in infants and children as midline or scar-associated protrusions.

## CLINICAL MANIFESTATIONS AND PATHOPHYSIOLOGY
An **epigastric hernia** is a small fascial defect (typically 0.5–1.5 cm) through the decussating fibers of the **linea alba between the xiphoid process and the umbilicus** (representing ~4–5% of pediatric abdominal wall hernias). Unlike umbilical hernias, epigastric hernias almost universally contain **extraperitoneal preperitoneal fat (properitoneal fat)** rather than bowel or omentum, present as a firm, pea-sized, sometimes tender subcutaneous midline nodule midway between the xiphoid and umbilicus that may be irreducible (incarcerated preperitoneal fat), and **DO NOT resolve spontaneously**. This must be distinguished from **diastasis recti** (a benign, wide, fusiform midline bulging along the entire linea alba from xiphoid to umbilicus during crying/straining without a discrete fascial ring, which resolves spontaneously without surgery). **Subchapter 420.1: Incisional Hernia** develops after1–3% of pediatric laparotomies due to wound infection, malnutrition, corticosteroid therapy, increased intra-abdominal pressure (ascites, peritoneal dialysis, giant omphalocele closure), or technical suture failure.

## DIAGNOSIS AND DIFFERENTIAL EVALUATION
Diagnosis of both **epigastric hernia** and **incisional hernia** is primarily **clinical** by palpating the discrete fascial defect along the linea alba or prior surgical scar with the child supine and standing/straining. High-resolution **linear abdominal wall ultrasound** confirms incarcerated preperitoneal fat vs a true hernia sac when physical examination is equivocal.

#### **Table 420.1** Key Clinical, Diagnostic, and Therapeutic Summary for Epigastric Hernia
| Clinical Entity / Parameter | Pathophysiology, Diagnosis & Management |
| --- | --- |
| **Epigastric Hernia** | Small defect in linea alba between xiphoid and umbilicus; contains preperitoneal fat; pea-sized midline mass; does NOT resolve spontaneously → elective surgical closure |
| **Diastasis Recti (Differential Diagnosis)** | Benign thinning/widening of linea alba without a discrete fascial defect; bulges only when crying/straining; 0% incarceration risk; reassurance only (no surgery) |
| **Umbilical Hernia (Differential Diagnosis)** | Patent umbilical ring; >85–90% close spontaneously by age 4–5 years; observe unless defect >1.5–2 cm persisting past age 4–5 yr or incarcerated |
| **Incisional Hernia (Subchapter 420.1)** | Fascial dehiscence at prior laparotomy scar (risk factors: wound infection, steroids, ascites); high incarceration risk in small defects → surgical repair |

## TREATMENT AND PROGNOSIS
Because **epigastric hernias do not close spontaneously** and frequently cause localized pain from fat incarceration, **elective outpatient surgical repair** (transverse skin incision over the mass, excision or reduction of the incarcerated preperitoneal fat, and primary interrupted suture closure of the linea alba defect) is recommended. **Incisional hernias** are repaired surgically via primary fascial closure (or prosthetic/biologic mesh reinforcement for large defects) once the child's nutritional and clinical status is optimized.
